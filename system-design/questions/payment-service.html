01What makes payments a different kind of system?

Before drawing a single box, it's worth being explicit about why this system doesn't play by the same rules as Instagram or WhatsApp.

In every system we've built so far, the worst-case failure was degraded experience — a stale feed, a duplicate notification, a slightly-late message. The fix was "retry, and if it happens twice, the user barely notices."

In a payment system, the worst-case failure is an incorrect fact about who owns how much money — and that fact gets printed on a bank statement, disputed by a customer, and audited by a regulator eighteen months later. "Retry and hope for the best" is not a strategy here — a retried charge can mean a real customer gets billed twice on a real credit card.

The core tension Payments are distributed transactions across systems you don't control — your database, a payment gateway (Stripe/Razorpay), a card network (Visa/Mastercard), and a bank — none of which share a transaction boundary. You cannot wrap "debit the card" and "credit the merchant" in a single ACID transaction. The entire design is about building strong consistency guarantees on top of a fundamentally unreliable, multi-party, asynchronous process.

This reframes the whole exercise. The questions that matter most in this HLD are not "how do we scale to a million QPS" (payment volumes are actually modest compared to social feeds) — they are:

  • What happens if the network call to the payment gateway times out — did the charge happen or not?
  • What happens if our server crashes after the gateway confirms the charge but before we record it?
  • What happens if the user double-clicks "Pay Now," or their flaky mobile network causes their client to retry the request?
  • How do we know, months later, that our internal ledger and the gateway's records actually agree?
Governing principle for this entire document Every operation that touches money must be idempotent, and every state transition must be durably recorded before it is acted upon. This is the payments-domain instance of the persist-first, push-second invariant we've used before — but here "push" isn't a notification, it's irreversible money movement, so the consequence of getting it backwards is not "a user is annoyed," it's "a customer is charged twice and a support ticket becomes a chargeback."

02What does this system actually need to do?

Functional Requirements

  1. Charge a payment method — a user pays a merchant a specific amount using a card, bank account, or wallet, and we get a definitive success/failure outcome.
  2. Refund a payment — full or partial, against a previously successful charge.
  3. Handle asynchronous outcomes — many payment methods (bank transfers, some card 3DS flows, wallets) don't resolve instantly; the system must track a payment that's "pending" and later transitions to success or failure via a webhook from the gateway.
  4. Never double-charge, never lose a charge — this is a requirement in its own right, not a "non-functional" add-on; it's the reason this system exists.
  5. Give merchants and users a reliable balance/history view — "what happened to my money" must be answerable at any time, consistently.

Non-Functional Requirements

  1. Correctness > latency > availability — in that order. This ordering is the single most important design constraint in this document and we'll return to it repeatedly. It's fine for a payment to take an extra 200ms. It is never fine for it to be wrong.
  2. Durability — once we've told a user "your payment succeeded," that fact can never be lost, even if every server in the fleet dies five milliseconds later.
  3. Auditability — every state transition needs an immutable, timestamped trail. Not for debugging convenience — for regulatory compliance (PCI-DSS) and dispute resolution.
  4. Idempotency under retry — the same logical request, sent N times due to client retries or network blips, must produce exactly one effect.
  5. Moderate scale, not massive scale — a large payment processor handles thousands of transactions per second at peak, not millions. This is a system where correctness engineering dominates over raw throughput engineering.
Why this ordering matters for the whole design Compare to Instagram, where we happily traded strict consistency for availability (eventually-consistent feeds, approximate like counts). Here we do the opposite: we will deliberately introduce latency and even reject requests rather than risk an incorrect financial state. A payment system that is "highly available but occasionally double-charges someone" is not a working payment system — it's a liability.

03Where do we draw the line?

Payment systems are notorious for scope creep in interviews — fraud detection alone is a full system design problem. Naming exclusions explicitly keeps the conversation focused on payment correctness.

In scope

  • Charge / capture / refund flow end-to-end
  • Idempotency guarantees under retry
  • Internal ledger design (double-entry)
  • Async settlement via webhooks
  • Reconciliation against the gateway
  • Failure handling: timeouts, crashes, partial failures

Below the line

  • Fraud / risk scoring engine (separate system, consumes our event stream)
  • PCI-DSS card vault implementation details (we assume a tokenization vault / gateway handles raw PAN storage — we never touch raw card numbers)
  • Currency conversion / FX rate management
  • Payout & settlement to merchant bank accounts (T+2 batch settlement is a downstream system)
  • Subscription / recurring billing scheduling logic
  • Tax calculation
A note on the vault exclusion We never store raw card numbers ourselves — that's what PCI-DSS Level 1 compliance from a processor (Stripe/Razorpay/Adyen) buys you. Our system stores tokens that represent a payment method, issued by the gateway. This is worth saying out loud in an interview even though it's "out of scope" — it shows you know the compliance boundary exists.

04What does the API contract look like?

The API surface itself encodes half of our correctness strategy — notice the idempotency key shows up before we've drawn a single architecture diagram. That's deliberate: it's a contract-level decision, not an implementation detail bolted on later.

POST /v1/payment_intents

  "currency": "INR",
  "payment_method_token": "pm_tok_9f8a...",
  "merchant_id": "mch_2210",
  "order_id": "ord_77123"
}

Response 201:
{
  "payment_intent_id": "pi_88f2...",
  "status": "requires_action" | "processing" | "succeeded" | "failed",
  "amount": 499900,
  "created_at": "2026-08-27T09:12:03Z"

EndpointPurposeIdempotent by design?
POST /v1/payment_intentsInitiate a chargeYes — client-supplied key
GET /v1/payment_intents/{id}Poll status (for async flows)Trivially (read-only)
POST /v1/refundsRefund a captured paymentYes — same key mechanism

Two design choices baked into the contract, worth narrating out loud 1. Amounts are integers in minor units (paise, cents) — never floating point. ₹4999.00 is transmitted as 499900. Floating point arithmetic on money is a classic junior mistake that produces off-by-one-paisa reconciliation nightmares at scale.

2. The response can be processing, not just success/failure — this signals from the API shape itself that we're modeling payments as asynchronous state machines, not synchronous RPC calls. We'll justify this heavily in Build-Up 3.

05What are the core entities, and why this shape?

EntityKey fieldsWhy it exists

A modeling decision that matters: payment_intent vs. ledger_entry are separate tables It's tempting to model this as one "transactions" table. We deliberately split state tracking (payment_intent — mutable, represents "where is this payment right now") from the financial record (ledger_entry — immutable, append-only, represents "what actually happened to the money"). A payment_intent can be updated as it moves through states. A ledger_entry, once written, is never updated or deleted — corrections happen via new offsetting entries, exactly like real double-entry bookkeeping. This distinction is one of the highest-signal things you can say unprompted in this interview — it shows you're not treating "database row" and "financial record" as the same concept.

Why Postgres (or another strongly-consistent RDBMS), not a NoSQL store? Recall from the ticketing and messaging systems — the answer to "what database" is never "NoSQL scales better," it's justified by access pattern and consistency needs. Here the justification is sharp: we need compare-and-swap semantics and multi-row ACID transactions (writing a payment_intent status update and its corresponding ledger entries atomically). A document store with eventual consistency would reintroduce exactly the race conditions we're trying to eliminate. This is a case where the CAP theorem trade-off is made explicitly in favor of consistency — we'd rather the write path be slightly slower and occasionally reject a request than risk two conflicting truths about the same money.

06Build-up 1 — what's the simplest thing that could possibly work?

Start naive on purpose. The failure mode we hit here is the entire reason idempotency exists, and walking through it concretely makes Build-Up 2 feel inevitable rather than arbitrary.

Every step here looks reasonable. The problem is step 2→3: the network in between.

Where this breaks

The request to the gateway (step 2) can fail in three distinct ways, and we cannot tell them apart from the client's perspective:

ScenarioWhat actually happenedWhat our server sees
Request never reached gatewayNo charge occurredTimeout
Request reached gateway, response lost on the way backCharge did occurTimeout
Gateway processed it, our server crashed before writing to PostgresCharge did occurOur DB has no record of it at all
The concrete failure User taps "Pay ₹4,999." Request reaches the gateway, card is charged, but the response packet is lost to a flaky mobile network. Our server times out and returns an error to the client. The client — reasonably — shows "Payment failed, please try again," and the user taps Pay again. The card is now charged twice, and neither our system nor the user has any idea until the credit card statement arrives. This is not a hypothetical — it is the single most common real-world payment bug, and it's structurally identical to the "timeout means unknown, not failure" lesson from distributed systems generally.

Two additional flaws worth naming even before we fix the timeout problem: this design has no durability boundary — if the process crashes between steps 3 and 4, we've lost all record that a real charge happened on a real card, and no protection against the client itself retrying a request it merely believes failed. We need a mechanism that makes retries safe by construction, not by hoping the network behaves.

07Build-up 2 — how do we make retries safe?

This is the first of the two or three decisions that actually matter in this system. Full mechanics are in Deep Dive 1 — here we establish why and get the architecture shape right.

Decision: How do we prevent a retried request from charging twice?
✗ Bad — Rely on the client not to retry
Approach Trust the mobile/web client to disable the "Pay" button after one tap and never resend. Why it fails Client-side guards are trivially bypassed (network layer retries below the UI, app crash-and-relaunch, a user with two tabs open). Client behavior is not something a financial system can depend on for correctness — the server must be the enforcement point.
△ Good — Deduplicate on business fields
Approach Before charging, check "has this order_id already been successfully charged?" — reject if so. Why it's incomplete Works for the simple "one charge per order" case, but breaks down the moment the domain gets richer — what about legitimate partial charges against the same order, or retries mid-flight where the first attempt is still processing and hasn't resolved yet? It conflates business-level dedup with request-level dedup, and the two aren't the same problem. It's also gateway-agnostic in the wrong direction — it protects our DB, but does nothing to prevent us from calling the gateway twice while the first call is still in flight.
✓ Great — Client-generated idempotency key, server-enforced, at the API boundary
Approach The client generates a UUID once per logical payment attempt (typically at "Pay button tapped") and sends it as an Idempotency-Key header on every retry of that same logical request — even across app restarts, because it's derived from something stable like the cart/order ID plus a client-side nonce. The server treats the key as the unit of "has this been done" and short-circuits duplicates before touching the gateway. Why it's the right layer This decouples "did the network fail" from "did the business operation happen." It works regardless of why the client is retrying — timeout, crash-relaunch, double-tap — because it dedupes on intent, not on transport behavior. And because the key is checked and locked before the gateway call (mechanics in Deep Dive 1), it prevents duplicate gateway calls entirely, not just duplicate DB writes.

The unique constraint on the idempotency_keys table is the actual mutex — see Deep Dive 1 for the full state machine including the "still in flight" case.

This fixes duplicate charges from retries. It does not yet fix the crash-after-gateway-success problem — if our process dies between "gateway said yes" and "we wrote that down," we still have an inconsistent state, just now bounded to a single occurrence instead of unbounded duplicate charges. And it doesn't yet give us an answer for payment methods that don't resolve synchronously at all. Two more problems to solve.

08Build-up 3 — how do we model money that doesn't move instantly?

UPI bank transfers, some 3D-Secure card flows, and most wallet top-ups don't give you a synchronous yes/no. The gateway says "processing" and tells you the real answer later via a webhook, sometimes seconds later, sometimes minutes. Our synchronous model from Build-Up 2 has no place to put this.

Decision: How do we handle payment methods that resolve asynchronously?
✗ Bad — Poll the gateway in a loop until it resolves
Approach Keep the client's HTTP connection open, poll the gateway's status endpoint every second, respond once resolved. Why it fails Ties up a server connection/thread for the entire duration (which can be minutes for bank transfers) — doesn't scale, and a client disconnect or our server restart mid-poll loses the entire operation with no record of where it left off.
△ Good — Client polls our status endpoint
Approach We return status: processing immediately; the client polls GET /payment_intents/{id} every few seconds until it flips to succeeded/failed. We ourselves poll the gateway server-side to update it. Why it's incomplete Better — connections aren't held open — but server-side polling of the gateway is wasteful (most polls return "still processing") and adds latency proportional to poll interval. It also doesn't establish the durable record-first discipline we need: if we're the one initiating the "did it resolve" question, we're still vulnerable to losing track of an intent if our poller crashes.
✓ Great — Model the payment as an explicit state machine; let the gateway push the resolution via webhook
Approach payment_intent.status is a real state machine (created → processing → succeeded/failed). We return processing immediately and stop actively waiting. The gateway calls us back on a webhook endpoint when it has a final answer. The client polls our lightweight status endpoint (cheap, reads our own DB, no gateway round-trip) or — better — we push the update over the existing WebSocket/push channel if the client is still connected. Why it's the right shape This inverts control correctly: the party with the actual information (the gateway) pushes it, rather than us guessing when to ask. It also means our system's correctness doesn't depend on any process staying alive for the duration of the payment — the webhook can land minutes later against a completely different server instance and the state machine handles it identically. This is the same "durable state + event-driven transition" shape we used for message delivery status in the WhatsApp system, applied to money instead of messages.

Notice: succeeded and failed are both terminal and both reached the same way — via webhook, never by our own optimistic assumption.

This solves the async-resolution problem, but introduces a new one worth naming immediately: webhooks are themselves unreliable — gateways retry webhook delivery on their end too (if we return a non-2xx or time out processing it), which means our webhook handler needs the exact same idempotency treatment we just built for the client-facing API. We'll fold that into the final design. It also raises the ledger question directly: once we know a payment succeeded, how do we record where the money is in a way that's auditable and can't silently drift? That's the double-entry ledger, covered fully in Deep Dive 2 — for now, the architectural commitment is: a payment_intent transitioning to succeeded is what triggers writing ledger entries, and that write happens in the same DB transaction as the status update.

09Build-up 4 — what if the webhook never arrives at all?

Webhooks fail silently: our endpoint could be down during a deploy, a load balancer misconfiguration could 404 it, or the gateway's own outage could drop it. If we rely purely on push, a payment can get stuck in processing forever with no signal that anything is wrong.

Concrete failure A user pays via UPI at 2:47 PM. Our webhook endpoint happens to be mid-deploy for 90 seconds and returns 503 to the gateway's callback. The gateway retries a few times with backoff, but if our deploy issue lasts longer than its retry window, the webhook is dropped for good. Our payment_intent sits in processing indefinitely. The user's bank account was actually debited. From the user's side: money left their account, and our app still says "processing" a week later.

Push alone is not a complete strategy for a financial system — it needs a pull-based safety net. This is where reconciliation enters: a background process that periodically asks the gateway "what's the real status of every payment intent I have that's been stuck in a non-terminal state for too long," and treats the gateway's answer as ground truth, correcting our own records if they've drifted. Full mechanics in Deep Dive 4.

Reframing what "source of truth" means here In most of the systems we've designed, our own database is the source of truth. In a payment system, that's subtly wrong: the payment gateway (and ultimately the card network / bank) is the actual source of truth about whether money moved. Our database is a (hopefully accurate, but not infallible) mirror of that truth, built via webhooks. Reconciliation exists specifically because a mirror can drift from its source, and in a financial system you cannot let that drift go undetected.

10Putting it all together — the final architecture

Solid arrows = synchronous call · dashed = async correction/replay path · gold = the idempotency and webhook dedup boundary that appears on both sides of the system

Notice the symmetry: the idempotency key pattern guards the client → us direction, and the webhook_events dedup table guards the gateway → us direction. Same underlying mechanism (deduplicate on a unique external identifier before doing work), applied at both boundaries where retries can occur. That's a pattern worth calling out explicitly in an interview — it shows the idempotency concept generalized correctly rather than being a one-off fix for the client API.

Why the outbox table, not "just publish to Kafka after the DB write" If we wrote the ledger entry to Postgres and then called Kafka's producer directly, we have a new dual-write problem: the DB commit can succeed and the Kafka publish can fail (or vice versa if ordered the other way), leaving downstream consumers (notifications, order fulfillment) out of sync with what actually happened financially. The transactional outbox pattern writes the "event to publish" row in the same DB transaction as the ledger entry — so it's atomic with the fact it describes — and a separate relay process reads the outbox table and publishes to Kafka, retrying the publish (safely, since publishing is itself made idempotent via the event ID) until it succeeds. This is the same persist-before-push principle from Section 1, applied one layer deeper.

11Deep Dive 1 — the idempotency key state machine, exactly

◆ Pattern: Idempotent Receiver
Worked mechanics

Section 7 established why we need idempotency keys. Here's precisely what the idempotency_keys table does and the exact race it has to close.

CREATE TABLE idempotency_keys (
  key TEXT PRIMARY KEY,
  request_hash TEXT NOT NULL, -- hash of the request body
  status TEXT NOT NULL, -- 'in_progress' | 'completed'
  response_body JSONB,
  locked_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
  expires_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL -- e.g. now() + 24h, matching Stripe's window
);

Step-by-step, for a fresh request with key idem_a1b2c3

  1. Attempt insert: INSERT INTO idempotency_keys (key, request_hash, status, locked_at, expires_at) VALUES ('idem_a1b2c3', hash(body), 'in_progress', now(), now()+'24h') ON CONFLICT (key) DO NOTHING
  2. If the insert succeeded (this request "won" the race) → proceed to call the gateway.
  3. If the insert conflicted (a row already exists for this key) → read the existing row and branch:
    • If status = 'completed' → return the cached response_body immediately. No gateway call. This is the "safe retry" case.
    • If status = 'in_progress' and locked_at is recent → return HTTP 409 Conflict. This means a request with the same key is currently executing — this is the concurrent double-click case, not a retry-after-completion case, and the correct answer is "reject, don't proceed," because we genuinely don't know the outcome yet.
    • If status = 'in_progress' and locked_at is stale (older than some threshold, e.g. 30s) → the previous attempt likely crashed mid-flight. Safe to re-acquire the lock and retry the gateway call ourselves (with the gateway call also being idempotent via a gateway-level idempotency key — see the note below).
    • If the incoming request body's hash doesn't match the stored request_hash → reject with an error. Same key, different payload, is a client bug (e.g. accidentally reusing a key across two different orders) and must never be silently accepted.
  4. After the gateway call resolves (success or definitive failure) → update the row: status='completed', response_body=<result>, in the same transaction as writing the payment_intent update.
The subtle bug this design prevents Without the in_progress / completed distinction, a naive "check if key exists, if not do work" approach has a race: two near-simultaneous requests with the same key can both pass the "does it exist" check before either has written a row, and both proceed to charge the gateway. The ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING insert is what makes the check-and-lock atomic — it's a single statement, so Postgres's own row-level locking does the mutex work for us instead of us trying to hand-roll a lock.

One more layer: idempotency at the gateway itself

Everything above protects our system. But what if we crash after sending the charge request to the gateway but before we get (or process) its response? We'd re-acquire the stale lock (case 4 above) and retry — but if we just resend the same call to the gateway, and the gateway did process the first one, we'd double charge at the gateway layer even though our own dedup worked perfectly. This is why we pass our own idempotency key through to the gateway as well — Stripe, Razorpay, and Adyen all support this natively. The client generates a key, and we forward it (or a deterministic derivative of it) as the gateway's own idempotency key, so the safety property holds transitively across the entire call chain, not just at our boundary.

Why the key must be client-generated, never server-generated If the server generates the idempotency key on receiving a request, then a client retry after a timeout — the exact scenario we're protecting against — arrives as a "new" request from the server's point of view (new key, because it's a new HTTP request), completely defeating the mechanism. The key has to represent the client's intent to pay, generated once, before the first attempt, and reused across every retry of that same intent. This is a one-sentence detail that separates people who've implemented this from people who've only read about it.

12Deep Dive 2 — why double-entry, and how the ledger actually works

◆ Pattern: Double-Entry Bookkeeping
Worked mechanics

A single-column "user balance" field is the single most common wrong answer here. It has no way to explain why a balance is what it is, and it's not resilient to partial failure — if the process crashes mid update, you have an unexplainable, unrecoverable number.

The invariant

Every financial transaction is recorded as at least two ledger entries that sum to zero — a debit from one account and a credit to another. This isn't accounting tradition for its own sake; it's a built-in correctness check: if the entries for any transaction don't sum to zero, you know immediately something is broken, without needing to reconstruct history from scratch.

-- User pays ₹4,999 to Merchant, we take a ₹150 platform fee
-- transaction_id: txn_9f2a1c groups these entries together

ledger_entry(txn_9f2a1c, account=user_wallet_772, DEBIT 499900)
ledger_entry(txn_9f2a1c, account=merchant_receivable_88, CREDIT 484900)
ledger_entry(txn_9f2a1c, account=platform_revenue, CREDIT 15000)

-- check: 499900 debit == 484900 + 15000 credit ✓ sums to zero

Why this beats a mutable balance column

PropertyMutable balance columnAppend-only double-entry ledger
Explains why balance is XNo — just a numberYes — replay all entries
Detects corruptionSilent drift possibleEntries not summing to zero is an alarm
Safe under concurrent writesNeeds row locks, race-proneAppend-only, no update contention
Auditable / regulator-readyHistory is destroyed on updateFull immutable history by construction
CorrectionsOverwrite (loses evidence)New offsetting entry (evidence preserved)

Current balance is then a derived value: SUM(credits) - SUM(debits) for a given account, which we materialize into a read-optimized balance cache (updated within the same transaction as the ledger write) rather than computing it live on every read — this is the same "durable write, cached read" shape we used for follower counts and message read receipts in earlier systems, just applied to money.

Edge case: partial refunds A ₹4,999 payment gets a ₹2,000 partial refund. This is not an update to the original ledger entries — it's a brand new transaction with its own entries: DEBIT platform_revenue/merchant_receivable 2000, CREDIT user_wallet 2000. The original transaction remains untouched, permanently reflecting "what actually happened at the time it happened." The user's current balance correctly reflects both transactions summed together. This is exactly how real accounting systems handle corrections, and it's why "just update the row" breaks down the moment refunds, partial refunds, or chargebacks enter the picture.

13Deep Dive 3 — orchestrating a payment that touches multiple systems

◆ Pattern: Saga (Orchestration)
Worked mechanics

A "simple" charge often isn't one step. Consider: reserve inventory → charge the card → confirm the order → release inventory reservation on failure. These span multiple services with no shared database, so we can't use a single ACID transaction across them — this is the exact same structural problem we solved with Postgres CAS in the ticketing system, but now spanning service boundaries instead of just rows.

Why orchestration over choreography here

Choreography (each service listens for events and reacts, no central coordinator) works well when steps are independent. Payments are the opposite case: the compensating actions must happen in a specific, deterministic order, and someone needs to own "what state is this multi-step operation currently in" as a single source of truth for retries and timeouts. That's a strong signal for an explicit orchestrator — the Payment Orchestrator box in the final architecture — rather than a web of services independently reacting to each other's events.

Note the direction of compensation: if step 2 (charge) fails, we compensate step 1 (release the inventory reservation) — we never need to compensate "charge" itself in this ordering, because charge is the failure point, not a prior success we need to undo. This is why step ordering in a saga is itself a design decision: putting the riskiest, most-likely-to-fail external call (charging a card) as early as possible in the sequence minimizes the number of compensating actions needed on the failure path. Reserving inventory is cheap and easy to undo; a charge that already succeeded is expensive and sometimes impossible to instantly undo (a refund is itself an async operation) — so we want charge to fail fast, before we've accumulated state that's harder to unwind.

Each saga step is independently idempotent The orchestrator itself can crash and restart mid-saga. When it resumes, it needs to safely retry whichever step it was on without re-doing already-completed steps or double-triggering compensations. This is why every step — including compensating actions — carries the same idempotency-key discipline from Deep Dive 1. The saga pattern doesn't replace idempotency; it's built entirely on top of it, one layer up.

14Deep Dive 4 — reconciliation: trusting the gateway over ourselves

◆ Pattern: Read-Repair / Anti-Entropy
Worked mechanics

Reconciliation is a scheduled batch job — not real-time, doesn't need to be — that closes the gap left by Build-Up 4's "webhook never arrives" problem. It runs, concretely, like this:

  1. Query: find all payment_intents where status IN ('processing', 'created') and updated_at is older than some threshold (e.g. 10 minutes — long enough that it's not just a normal in-flight payment, short enough to catch problems same-day).
  2. For each stuck intent, call the gateway's GET /charges/{gateway_ref} status endpoint directly — bypassing webhooks entirely, asking the source of truth.
  3. Compare:
    • Gateway says succeeded, we have processing → we missed the webhook. Replay the exact same "webhook received" logic manually, writing ledger entries and transitioning state — using the gateway event ID as the dedup key so this is safe even if the original webhook eventually arrives late.
    • Gateway says failed, we have processing → transition to failed, no ledger entries needed.
    • Gateway says succeeded, and we also say succeeded, but the amounts don't match → do not auto-correct. Flag for manual review. This is the one case where automated reconciliation should refuse to act — a mismatch this specific usually indicates a bug or fraud, not a timing issue, and silently "fixing" the ledger here is how real accounting errors get compounded.
The failure mode reconciliation itself must avoid Reconciliation calling the gateway for every stuck intent, naively, is itself a retry storm risk if a large batch of webhooks gets dropped at once (e.g. during an incident). The job needs its own rate limiting and backoff against the gateway's API, and should process the backlog gradually rather than firing thousands of status checks in the same second — the same thundering-herd concern we handled with jittered retries and cache warming in earlier systems, showing up again here in a different guise.
Sizing the reconciliation job At 2,000 payments/sec peak, ~99.9% resolve via webhook within 30 seconds. That leaves ~0.1% × 2,000/sec × 86,400 sec/day ≈ 172,800 stuck intents/day in the worst realistic case — but most of those resolve on their own within the 10-minute grace window before reconciliation even looks at them. In practice, genuinely-stuck intents needing gateway lookups are a much smaller number — low thousands/day for a mid-size processor — well within a single background worker polling the gateway's status API with modest concurrency and backoff.

15Edge cases worth being able to talk through unprompted

These are the questions that separate "I described the happy path well" from "I've actually thought about what breaks." Each maps to a mechanism already built above.

Edge caseWhat breaks naivelyHow the design handles it
User double-taps "Pay" Two charges for the same purchase Client reuses same idempotency key → second request short-circuits at the DB unique constraint (Deep Dive 1)
Network timeout mid-charge Client retries blindly, charge may have already succeeded Idempotency key ensures retry returns the cached original outcome, never re-executes
Our server crashes after gateway confirms, before we persist We have no record a real charge happened; user's card was debited with zero trace on our side Reconciliation job discovers the drift by asking the gateway directly and self-heals
Webhook delivered twice by the gateway (gateways retry webhook delivery on non-2xx) Ledger entries written twice → balance corruption webhook_events table dedups on gateway's own event ID before processing
Partial refund after a partial refund already happened Refunding more than was actually paid Refund amount validated against SUM(original) - SUM(prior refunds) from the ledger, not a cached balance
Currency mismatch between request and payment method Silent miscalculation if treated as same-currency Explicitly out of scope (FX) but the API rejects mismatched currency rather than silently converting
Chargeback / dispute filed weeks later No clean way to reflect "money we thought we had is being clawed back" Modeled as a new transaction type with its own ledger entries — same append-only mechanism as refunds, different trigger source (gateway-initiated, not merchant-initiated)
Concurrent requests with the same idempotency key arrive simultaneously Race condition, both might proceed to charge Second request sees status='in_progress' and fresh locked_at → returns 409, doesn't proceed (Deep Dive 1, case 3)
Gateway itself has an outage during our call Indistinguishable from a plain timeout without careful handling Treated identically to any timeout — idempotency key means it's always safe to retry later once the gateway recovers
Clock skew between our server and the gateway affecting "stuck" detection Reconciliation fires too early/late Threshold uses our own updated_at, not gateway timestamps — we only compare state, never rely on cross-system clock agreement

16How big does this actually need to be?

Deliberately modest scale compared to the social/messaging systems — the point here is that correctness engineering, not raw throughput, is what makes this system hard.

Assumptions: mid-size payment processor 50M transactions/day
Average ≈ 50M / 86,400s ≈ 580 QPS, peak (flash sale / holiday) ≈ 5–10× ≈ 3,000–6,000 QPS

Storage (ledger, dominant cost) Each transaction → ~2.5 ledger entries on average (debit + credit + occasional fee split)
50M × 2.5 = 125M entries/day, ~300 bytes/entry ≈ 37.5 GB/day → ~13.7 TB/year
Manageable on Postgres with time-based partitioning; cold partitions (>1 year) archived to object storage, since ledger data is append-only and never updated — a perfect fit for partition-and-archive.

Idempotency key table 580 QPS × 86,400s ≈ 50M keys/day, but with a 24-hour TTL the table never holds more than ~1 day's worth ≈ 50M rows steady-state, small (<100 bytes/row) → ~5 GB, easily kept hot with an index on key and a background job pruning expires_at < now().

Why this doesn't need to be "webscale": compare 580 QPS average here to the hundreds of thousands of QPS a social feed read path handles. The engineering effort in this system goes almost entirely into correctness (idempotency, sagas, reconciliation) rather than horizontal read scaling — a useful thing to say explicitly, because it signals you're allocating design effort to where the actual difficulty lives, not defaulting to a scaling narrative because that's the template.

17What should you actually say, unprompted, at each level?

Mid-Level

  • Draws the client → payment service → gateway → DB flow correctly
  • Knows payments need a database and a "status" field
  • Mentions "we should handle failures" in general terms, without a concrete mechanism
  • May reach for "just retry" without recognizing that's the exact source of the double-charge bug

Senior

  • Proactively raises idempotency keys as the mechanism for safe retries, and gets the client-generated-key detail right
  • Recognizes payments as an async state machine, not a synchronous RPC — introduces webhooks unprompted
  • Chooses a strongly-consistent DB and justifies it on transactional/CAS grounds, not by reflex
  • Names PCI-DSS / tokenization as a compliance boundary even while scoping it out

Staff+

  • Leads with the reframe: this system's hard problem is correctness-under-failure, not scale — and orders the NFRs accordingly (correctness > latency > availability)
  • Introduces double-entry ledger design unprompted, and explains why a mutable balance column is the wrong primitive, not just that it's "less good"
  • Identifies that webhooks alone are an incomplete strategy and proactively designs the reconciliation loop as the pull-based safety net for push-based delivery
  • Recognizes idempotency as a pattern that must be applied at every boundary where retries can occur (client API, gateway calls, webhook receipt, saga steps) — not bolted onto one endpoint
  • Distinguishes "our database" from "the actual source of truth" (the gateway/card network) and designs specifically to detect and correct drift between them
  • Reasons explicitly about saga step ordering — putting the highest-risk, hardest-to-compensate step (the charge) early to minimize compensation surface on failure
  • Ties capacity numbers back to a design decision — e.g. using the reconciliation queue-depth math to justify why it can be a modest background worker, not a scaled-out service
