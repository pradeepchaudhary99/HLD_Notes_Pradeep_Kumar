CAP Theorem & Consistency Models

Week 1Theory

CAP theorem

In a distributed system, when a network partition occurs, you must choose between Consistency and Availability. You cannot have all three of Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance simultaneously — and in practice, partitions will happen, so the real choice is C vs A during a partition.

Consistency (C)

Every read receives the most recent write or an error. All nodes see the same data at the same time.

Availability (A)

Every request receives a (non-error) response, without the guarantee that it contains the most recent write.

Partition tolerance (P)

The system continues to operate despite an arbitrary number of messages being dropped or delayed between nodes by the network.

Why P isn't optional

Networks fail. In any real distributed system, you must tolerate partitions — so CAP is really "when a partition happens, pick C or A."

Fig 1. The CAP triangle — real distributed systems live on the C↔P or A↔P edge, since P is mandatory once a network is involved.
ChoiceBehavior during partitionExamples
CPReject/block requests on the minority side to guarantee consistencyMongoDB (default), HBase, Zookeeper, etcd, most RDBMS with sync replication
APKeep serving reads/writes on both sides; reconcile later (risk of stale/conflicting data)Cassandra, DynamoDB, Riak, CouchDB
Interview framing
Don't just say "I'll use Cassandra because it's AP." Tie it to the requirement: "Our feed can tolerate a follower seeing a slightly stale like-count for a few seconds, but the checkout service cannot double-charge a card — so I'd pick AP for the feed and CP for payments."

PACELC — the more complete picture

CAP only describes behavior during a partition. PACELC extends it: if Partitioned, choose Availability or Consistency; Else (normal operation, no partition), choose Latency or Consistency. This matters because even without a partition, synchronous replication for strong consistency adds latency.

  • PA/EL systems (e.g. Cassandra, DynamoDB): favor availability during a partition and low latency normally, at the cost of consistency in both cases.
  • PC/EC systems (e.g. traditional RDBMS with synchronous replication, Spanner-like systems): favor consistency always, accepting higher latency and reduced availability during partitions.

Consistency models (from strongest to weakest)

Fig 2. The consistency spectrum — pick the weakest model that still satisfies your correctness requirement.

Strong / Strict consistency

Every read reflects the latest committed write, and all clients agree on ordering as if all operations happened on a single machine with no delay. Extremely expensive — usually requires consensus (Paxos/Raft) or a single writer.

Linearizability

A specific, well-defined form of strong consistency: every operation appears to take effect instantaneously at some point between its start and end, and once a write completes, every subsequent read (by any client) sees that write or a later one. It's "real-time" consistent — no client can ever observe a value going "backwards." Systems like etcd, Zookeeper, and Spanner (via TrueTime) provide this. It's expensive: typically requires consensus on every write and coordination across replicas.

Sequential consistency

All operations appear in some global order that's consistent with the order each individual client issued its own operations — but that order doesn't have to match real/wall-clock time. Weaker (and cheaper) than linearizability, still strong enough for many distributed algorithms.

Causal consistency

Operations that are causally related (e.g. a comment that replies to a post) are seen by everyone in the same order; unrelated (concurrent) operations can be seen in different orders by different clients. Common in collaborative and social apps — you never see a reply before the comment it replies to.

Eventual consistency

If no new writes occur, all replicas will eventually converge to the same value — but there's no bound promised on how long "eventually" takes, and reads in the meantime can return stale or even out-of-order data. Cheapest, most available, most scalable. Used by DNS, Cassandra, DynamoDB, S3 (historically), CDN caches.

Read-your-writes & monotonic reads
Two practical eventual-consistency refinements interviewers love to probe: read-your-own-writes (a user always sees their own update immediately, even if others don't yet) and monotonic reads (once you've seen a value, you never see an older one on subsequent reads). Both are achievable with sticky sessions or version vectors without paying for full strong consistency.
